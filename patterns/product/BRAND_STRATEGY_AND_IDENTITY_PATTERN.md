# Brand Strategy, Naming & Identity Pattern

> **Module**: M00 (Product Discovery), M01 (Idea Feasibility), M04 (UI/UX & Prototyping)
> **Source**: Adapted from a published brand-building-skills library (MIT) — positioning discipline, naming workflow, brand audit scorecard, brand architecture models, rebrand scoping, and brand measurement funnel.
> **Scope**: The strategy layer that sits *above* the logo. A mark cannot be judged without a position, a position cannot be owned without a name, and neither survives without a measurement system. This pattern covers positioning, naming, architecture, audit, rebrand, and measurement — in that dependency order.

---

## 1. Why This Exists

M00 asks for a value proposition and a moat. M04 asks for a logo, a palette, and a voice. Nothing in between says **what the brand actually owns in the customer's mind**, or **how to choose a name that can carry that ownership**. The result is predictable: a well-executed logo for an undifferentiated position, and a name chosen because it "sounds nice".

Brand work is not decoration. It is a series of narrowing decisions: category → audience → territory → name → voice → identity. Each depends on the one before it.

---

## 2. Positioning: The Territory

### 2.1 Positioning is not "better", it is "different"

A position is the mental space a brand occupies. Being better is a comparison a competitor can answer; being *different in a way that matters* is a position they would have to abandon their own strategy to copy.

A well-positioned brand:
- owns a specific idea or territory in its category
- is the obvious choice for a clearly defined audience
- cannot be confused with alternatives
- can say **no** to opportunities outside its territory

### 2.2 Category challenge (run this first)

Most positioning fails because the brand picks the wrong category to compete in.

| Layer | Question | Example |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Stated category** | What would the brand say it is? | "A project management tool" |
| **Actual category** | What does the customer compare it against when deciding? | "A spreadsheet plus a WhatsApp group" |
| **Opportunity category** | Is there an adjacent framing the brand could own outright? | "A client-facing status page generator" |

**Recommendation**: pick the framing that gives the strongest *ownable* position, not the one that sounds most impressive. A brand that redefines its category to something smaller and sharper usually wins more than one that claims a bigger, crowded category.

### 2.3 The positioning statement

```
For [specific target audience],
[Brand] is the [category]
that [key differentiator / benefit]
because [reason to believe].
```

Write three versions:
1. **The formal statement** — internal alignment.
2. **The strategic version** — sharper, for planning documents.
3. **The human version** — what a person would actually say to a friend.

If the formal and the human version disagree, the position is not real yet.

### 2.4 Proof points and refusals

A position needs two things to be credible:

- **4–5 proof points** — facts, features, or evidence that make the claim believable. A position without proof is a slogan.
- **3–4 explicit refusals** — "not for people who want X", "will never compete on Y". Refusals are what make a position sharp; a position that excludes nobody attracts nobody.

### 2.5 Positioning red flags

| Red flag | Why it fails |
| :--- | :--- |
| Could describe any competitor in the category | It is a category description, not a position |
| Audience too broad ("everyone who wants quality") | Cannot be owned; no channel reaches "everyone" |
| Differentiator is a table stake ("great customer service") | Every competitor claims it; it differentiates nothing |
| No proof points | Unfalsifiable claim; customers discount it |
| Territory requires a capability the brand lacks | Position is aspirational, not actual |

## 3. Naming: The Verbal Anchor

### 3.1 Modes

- **Generation** — a brief exists, names are needed.
- **Evaluation** — names exist, a decision is needed.
- **Both** — generate, then evaluate the user's own candidates alongside.

### 3.2 The naming strategy brief (before any name)

```
Brand in one line:
Audience the name must attract:
Positioning angle the name must fit:
Emotional territory the name should create:
3–5 naming directions to explore:
Themes/words to explore:
Themes/words to avoid:
```

A name generated without this brief is a lottery ticket.

### 3.3 The seven tests

Apply all seven to every candidate. The first three are pass/fail for most names.

| # | Test | Question |
| :-: | :--- | :--- |
| 1 | **Sound** | Said aloud, does it feel natural? |
| 2 | **Memory** | Remembered after hearing it once? |
| 3 | **Search** | Can someone spell it after hearing it once? |
| 4 | **Story** | Can the founder explain why this name exists? |
| 5 | **Logo** | Can it become a strong visual identity? |
| 6 | **Expansion** | Can the brand grow under it, or does it lock it to one product? |
| 7 | **Trust** | Does it feel credible for this category at this price point? |

### 3.4 Banned name shapes

Overused AI/startup suffixes unless strategically justified: `-ly`, `-ify`, `-io`, `-hub`, `-labs`, `-works`. Also banned: names that are one letter away from a competitor, names with unintended meanings in the target market's language, and names that sound like a cheap SaaS tool when the brand is not one.

**Indonesia-specific**: check the name for unintended meanings in Bahasa Indonesia *and* the relevant regional language (Javanese, Sundanese, Balinese) before shortlisting. A name that is elegant in English can be embarrassing in one market.

### 3.5 Evaluation scoring

Score each name 1–10 on **clarity, memory, sound, visual potential**, plus a **risk** rating (low/med/high) for domain, trademark, pronunciation, and associations. Never present a ranked list without the risk column — a strong name with a high trademark risk is not a recommendation.

### 3.6 Pre-finalisation checklist (never skip)

- [ ] Domain availability (`.com` + `.id` for Indonesian brands)
- [ ] Trademark check in the category and market
- [ ] Social handle availability
- [ ] Said aloud — does it feel natural?
- [ ] Spelled correctly by someone who only heard it once
- [ ] No unintended meaning in the target market's language

> The agent can shortlist and strategise. **Legal clearance and trademark registration are not agent work** (`docs/AUTHORITY.md`).

## 4. Architecture: When There Is More Than One Brand

Relevant from the second product onward. Four models, each with a real cost:

| Model | Shape | Best when | Cost |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Branded house** | One master brand; products are descriptors | Strong parent, consistent audience, want one brand to compound | One failure damages everything |
| **House of brands** | Independent brands; parent invisible | Very different audiences or positions | Every brand needs its own marketing budget |
| **Endorsed** | Sub-brands with parent endorsement | New market where parent credibility helps but differentiation is needed | Muddled middle — neither independent nor unified |
| **Hybrid** | Different models for different parts | Mature, complex portfolio | Requires explicit rules about which product follows which model |

**The question architecture answers**: *"When we launch something new, where does it fit?"* Without a rule, every launch invents a new convention.

For each element, decide **keep / update / replace** with a stated rationale — name, mark, palette, typography, tagline, positioning, audience, voice.

## 5. Audit: Diagnosing Before Rebuilding

Run this before any rebrand, and whenever the brand "feels off" but nobody can say why.

Score six dimensions **1–5**:

| Dimension | What it measures | Diagnostic question |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Positioning clarity** | Is it obvious who this is for and why it differs? | Could this positioning apply to a competitor? |
| **Visual consistency** | Logo, palette, type used coherently? | Are three or more logo versions in active use? |
| **Messaging consistency** | Same core message across site, social, deck? | Do homepage hero, social bio, and pitch deck say the same thing? |
| **Voice consistency** | Distinctive, recognisable writing? | With the logo removed, could you identify the brand from the copy? |
| **Audience alignment** | Speaking to a defined audience in their language? | Does the brand solve a problem the audience actually has? |
| **Competitive differentiation** | Immediately distinct from the top three? | Is there a clear reason to choose it over alternatives? |

Output: a **scorecard out of 30**, then **what's working** (specific, not "good logo"), **what's not working** (issue + evidence + impact, ranked), and **recommendations** split into immediate / 30–60 days / strategic.

A dimension scoring 1–2 on positioning cannot be fixed by a visual refresh. That is the single most common and most expensive brand mistake.

## 6. Rebrand: Scope Before Design

### 6.1 The honest diagnosis

Do not accept "we need a fresh look". Push for the trigger: new audience, new market, new leadership, new product, reputation issue, merger, or an identity that is genuinely dated. The trigger determines the scope.

### 6.2 Four rebrand types — pick exactly one

| Type | What changes | Use when |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Refresh** | Visual update only | Positioning is right; execution is dated |
| **Repositioning** | Position, audience, or market (may include visuals) | The brand is aimed at the wrong people or the wrong category |
| **Full rebrand** | Name, identity, positioning, messaging | The old brand actively blocks the strategy |
| **Extension** | New identity for a new product line under the existing brand | Portfolio growth, not brand change |

### 6.3 Equity audit before scope

Classify every existing asset as **has equity** (recognition or positive association worth preserving), **working**, **broken**, or **neutral**. A rebrand that discards equity is a new brand with an old brand's payroll.

### 6.4 Transition narrative

Write three versions, each with a different audience and length:
- **Customers** (150–200 words): what changes, why, what stays, what it means for them.
- **Employees** (100–150 words): why it matters, their role, what to say when asked.
- **Press/public** (200–300 words): the evolution story, why now, where it is going.

A rebrand without an internal narrative leaks confusion to customers.

## 7. Measurement: Proving the Brand Works

Brand metrics are lagging indicators. The system must contain both leading signals (momentum, visible in weeks) and lagging signals (business impact, visible in quarters).

### 7.1 The funnel

| Stage | Metric | How |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Awareness** | Unaided / aided awareness, share of voice, direct traffic, branded search volume | Survey + Search Console |
| **Consideration** | Consideration rate, NPS, return visitor rate | Survey + analytics |
| **Preference** | Win rate vs named competitors, preference score, premium pricing ability | Sales data + survey |
| **Loyalty** | Retention, LTV, referral rate, unprompted mentions | Product data + social listening |

### 7.2 Perception

Sentiment across social/review/press; **brand associations** (open-ended: "what three words describe this brand?") tracked quarterly for drift against intended personality; trust score.

### 7.3 The equity scorecard

Score **1–5** on awareness, differentiation, relevance, trust, preference, loyalty — quarterly or bi-annually. **Direction matters more than the absolute score.** A brand at 3/5 rising beats a brand at 4/5 falling.

### 7.4 Connecting brand to business

| Horizon | Proxy |
| :--- | :--- |
| **3–6 months** | Lower CAC, higher organic share, better organic conversion |
| **6–18 months** | Shorter sales cycle, higher competitive win rate, improving NPS |
| **18+ months** | Premium pricing ability, lower churn, compounding CAC advantage |

Present these as *proxies with stated lags*, never as direct attribution. Claiming "brand caused +12% revenue" without a control is a claim the data cannot support.

## 8. Scale Application

| Scale | Apply | Skip |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Small / MVP** | §2.3 positioning statement (one line), §3.3 seven tests at naming time, §7.1 awareness + loyalty only | Audit scorecard, architecture, rebrand framework, perception tracking |
| **Solo SaaS** | + §2.2 category challenge, §2.4 proof points, §7.1 full funnel, §7.3 scorecard | Architecture (single brand), rebrand framework |
| **Client Medium** | Full §2–§7; audit before any rebrand; transition narrative for rebrand work | Architecture unless the client has a portfolio |
| **Large** | Full set + §4 architecture + quarterly perception tracking + §6.4 all three narratives | — |
| **Enterprise** | Routed to A00–A04; this pattern supplies the vocabulary the advisory pack cites, but brand governance artifacts are A-series deliverables | — |

## 9. Failure Modes

1. **Logo-first branding** — designing a mark before the position exists, then retrofitting a story onto it.
2. **Category inflation** — claiming a bigger category than the brand can win, to sound more important.
3. **Name by committee** — a name everyone tolerates, that nobody can remember or spell.
4. **Refusal-free positioning** — a position that excludes nobody, therefore attracts nobody.
5. **Refresh-as-rebrand** — changing visuals to avoid an uncomfortable strategic conversation.
6. **Equity amnesia** — discarding a recognised asset because the team is bored of it.
7. **Attribution theatre** — claiming brand caused a revenue change the measurement cannot isolate.
8. **Invented audience language** — writing "their words" without any real quote behind it (violates M00's evidence rule).
